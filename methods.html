Methods

A blink comparator is only as good as its discipline. These are the exact decisions SkyBlink makes between the archive and your screen — including the two mistakes we caught and fixed during the build.

The pipeline

  1. Discover. IRSA's SIA service is queried for every SPHEREx observation intersecting the field, restricted to the current uniform releases: DR1 and QR3 (collections spherex_dr1, spherex_dr1_deep, spherex_qr3, spherex_qr3_deep). The superseded QR2 processing is never mixed in.
  2. Cluster & choose. Observations are clustered into epochs (gaps over 20 days). The archive returns frames whose footprints merely intersect the search circle, so for each epoch we keep the frame whose centre is closest to the target — across all six detectors, because the detectors image offset strips of sky and forcing one detector loses whole epochs.
  3. Cut out. Each chosen exposure is fetched as an archive cutout centred on the target, following the IRSA tutorial notebooks' recipe.
  4. Align. Cutouts are reprojected (Astropy + reproject) onto one shared north-up tangent-plane grid at 3.1″/pixel for the target.
  5. One stretch. A single percentile + asinh brightness stretch is computed across all epochs of a target and applied identically — so brightness differences between frames are the sky changing, not the scaling.
  6. Date honestly. Epoch dates come from the archive query's observation times, never from file-processing timestamps in FITS headers.

Two mistakes we caught — and how

  1. The upside-down sky. Reprojection arrays run from low to high declination, so our first renders were south-up. We caught it by checking a rendered frame against the Gaia-propagated position of Barnard's Star: the star sat mirrored. Frames are now flipped to true north-up, verified both numerically (centroid within ~1 px of the WCS prediction) and visually (the difference dipole points along the known proper-motion direction).
  2. The 4.4-arcminute stale centre. Barnard's Star moves so fast that its catalogue J2000 position is ~4.4′ away from where it is now — outside a 5′ frame's centre. SkyBlink centres it on its Gaia DR3 position propagated to the survey era; the same propagation powers the Gaia overlay, so reference stars land on their epoch-correct positions in every frame.

The Two-Epoch Check

A candidate mover is promoted only if it (1) appears in both epochs, shifted consistently; (2) is not a known Gaia star at the new position, with Gaia positions proper-motion propagated to the epoch; and (3) survives the difference view as a clean dipole. Cosmic rays, ghosts and saturation artefacts fail at least one test by construction. Full case treatment on the Case Files page.

Relation to WiseView

The challenge points builders at WiseView, the established WISE blink tool, and SkyBlink deliberately follows its proven interaction grammar — blink interval, epoch stepping, difference imaging, percentile stretch — while differing where the data differs: SPHEREx instead of WISE, 102 bands across six detectors instead of two, DR1 + QR3 multi-epoch sets up to a year apart, and a Gaia overlay propagated per epoch. Where WiseView helped us, we say so.

Stated limits

  1. WISE 0855 is intrinsically near-invisible below ~3 µm; its short-band frames are shown as honest non-detections, not enhanced into a “signal”.
  2. Barnard's Star saturates its core in SPHEREx exposures; centroiding uses the unsaturated wings, which is why Case 3 quotes arcsecond-level tolerances.
  3. Band fluxes are aperture sums on rendered frames — indicative, not a calibrated spectrum; the per-pixel spectral WCS route is the documented next step.
  4. The pre-loaded site covers the five official targets plus the 3I/ATLAS case; arbitrary-coordinate rendering runs on the same pipeline in the project repository's API service.
